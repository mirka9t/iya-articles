WhyTeenagers at Puberty Age Have a Strong Desire to Date: A CriticalExamination

Mirjahon Mamatkarimov Sep 12, 2026

Introduction

Somewhere around age 12 or 13, the majority of teenagers start wanting a boyfriend or a girlfriend. This is the one of the most common things that happen with teenagers when growing up and nobody questions this seriously and quote it as “normal”. Parents expect it but in our culture and mentality most families try to teach their kids that it is not good to be involved in relationships or playing out with girls or boys. Schools see it every day and some schools ofcourse punish or inform parents but most dont take it seriously. Movies and shows treat it like a natural step in life and normalize it. But here is the thing that many people neglect. Dating as we know it today did not exist 200 years ago, even if, it was shame. The word date was first used in 1896 in a newspaper column. Before that, people got married through family arrangements, and love had very little to do with any of it. In religion, men went to the brother and mostly to the father of the girl and asked for her hand, if both of them agreed they were married without long delays. Today however, people get married almost under 30 after almost decade of dating.

So why do teenagers feel such a strong push to date and desire to be together with other gender. Is it just hormones? Is it society? Is it because everyone around them is doing it? And more importantly do they actually need to date at all.

This paper looks at where dating came from, what happens inside a teenager's brain during puberty, and why the desire to date is so strong at that age. It also asks a harder question: is teenage dating actually good for teenagers or does it cause more harm than good. The goal here is to use real data and real studies and real numbers and to challenge the idea that teenage dating is just normal and therefore fine.

According to Pew Research Center, about 35% of teenagers aged 13 to 17 have some kind of romantic experience. By age 17 that number jumps to 78%. The average age of a first date in the U.S. is around 12.5 years old. These numbers tell us that this is a massive part of teenage life. But the fact that something is common does not mean it is healthy or necessary.

A Brief History of Dating

Here is something most teenagers do not realize. Dating is a modern invention which was later created by evolution of society. For most of human history, it did not exist or even if, there wasn't the idea of "we are couples”. People did not go on dates. They did not choose who they married either. Their parents did mostly, which is also arguable.

Before the 19th century, marriages were arranged. Families looked at money and/or land and social class and political connections. Love was not part of the equation. A father would pick a husband for his daughter based on what the family would gain from the deal. This was true in Europe, Asia, the Middle East, and most other places around the world. Mostly and arguably, marriage was a kind of business transaction, and very rarely, a love story.

Things started to shift in the early 1800s. In the United States and parts of Europe, young people slowly began choosing their own partners. But even then, the process was very controlled. A man would come to a woman's house as a "gentleman caller." He would sit in the family parlor and talk to the woman while her parents watched and then leave. There was no going out or there was no alone time. So everything between them happened under supervision.

The real change came in the 1920s. With cities growing and women working in factories and cars becoming available young people could actually go places without their parents. They could go to restaurants and movie theaters and parks and dance halls. This is when dating as a word and as a practice was born. By the mid 1920s, the old system of supervised courtship was basically dead because no body no longer cared.

But here is what is important. Dating in the 1920s was not about love either. It was about popularity. Who could get the most dates? Who was seen with the most attractive people? Dating became a social competition, especially on college and uni campuses. Feelings wasnt the main thing here, It was about their status.

In the 1950s, dating shifted again. Going steady which means being exclusive with one person became the norm. Then the 1960s brought the sexual revolution and birth control pills and a much more relaxed attitude toward relationships. By the time we got to the 2000s and 2010s dating apps changed everything once more. Now there are tonns of them.

The point is this. Dating is not a natural human behavior. It is a cultural invention that is barely 100 years old. Humans lived, loved, and got married for thousands of years without it. The idea that teenagers need to date is not based on biology. It is based on a culture that was created very recently.

What Puberty Does to theBrain

Let us talk about what is actually happening inside a teenager's body when they hit puberty. Because this part is real. It is biology and it is powerful.

Puberty starts when the brain sends signals to the body to produce sex hormones. In boys, the testes start making about 20 times more testosterone than before. In girls, the ovaries increase estrogen production by about 6 times. These are massive changes. They do not just change the body. They change the brain too.

Testosterone and estrogen trigger sexual thoughts, desire and feelings of attraction. Before puberty most kids do not think about romance, even if, it is because they are curious, but usually they are not involved excessively. After puberty starts it becomes hard to think about anything else. And this is not the choice of them, It is anatomical chemistry inside neurons.

But it goes deeper than sex hormones. When a teenager develops a crush or falls in love the brain releases dopamine. That is the same chemical that makes you feel good when you eat your favorite food or win a game. Because attraction involves positive reinforcement, which is a big topic in psychology. Brain scans done by researchers at University College London showed that when teenagers look at photos of someone they like four areas of their brain light up. Two are in the cortex and two are deep inside the brain. The pattern looks almost the same as the brain of someone on cocaine. That is how strong the chemical reaction is.

There is also oxytocin which is sometimes called the cuddle hormone. It gets released through physical touch - holding hands, hugging, kissing. It creates a feeling of closeness and bonding. For teenagers who have never felt this before, it is overwhelming. Even tiny moments of contact, like brushing someone's hand, can trigger a big oxytocin release.

Here is the critical part. During adolescence the baseline levels of dopamine are actually lower than in adults. But when something triggers a dopamine release the amount released is much higher. This means teenagers feel the highs more intensely than adults do. A text from a crush feels like the best thing that has ever happened. Being ignored by that same person feels like the end of the world.

On top of all this the prefrontal cortex which is the part of the brain responsible for rational thinking and planning and impulse control is not fully developed until around age 25. So teenagers are dealing with adult-level hormones and drug-level dopamine hits but with a brain that cannot fully process or control these emotions. That is a rough combination.

So yes the desire to be close to someone during puberty is real and biological. But and this is the key point the desire for closeness is not the same thing as the desire to date. Biology makes teenagers attracted to people. Culture tells them to turn that attraction into a relationship.

WhyTeenagers Actually Want to Date

Hormones explain the attraction. But they do not explain the dating. Attraction and dating are two different things actually. You can be attracted to someone without going on a date with them. You can love someone without making it a labeled relationship. So why do teenagers turn feelings into "relationships"?

There are several reasons, and most of them have nothing to do with love.

Identity. Adolescence is the time when people figure out who they are. Psychologist Erik Erikson called this the "identity vs. role confusion" stage. Teenagers are asking themselves: Who am I. Am I attractive. Am I interesting. Am I worth something. Having a boyfriend or girlfriend becomes a way to answer these questions. If someone likes me then I must be okay. The relationship becomes a mirror. A tool for self-validation.

Loneliness. Puberty is emotionally isolating. Teenagers start pulling away from their parents but do not yet have a stable adult identity. They are stuck in between. A romantic partner fills that gap. It gives them someone who is theirs. Someone they feel closer to than friends or family. The relationship becomes an emotional crutch.

Status. In many schools and social groups, having a boyfriend or girlfriend raises your social standing. A 2024 survey by the Survey Center on American Life found that 44% of Gen Z men never had a romantic relationship during their teen years, and many of them reported feeling like outsiders because of it. Dating is a social currency. Not dating makes you feel like you are falling behind somehow.

Curiosity. Teenagers are naturally curious. They want to experience everything. Dating is new and exciting, and it comes with all the drama and intensity that a teenage brain craves. Remember dopamine levels spike higher in teenagers than in adults. New experiences feel more rewarding and a relationship is the ultimate new experience.

Imitation. Teenagers copy what they see. If their friends are dating they want to date too. If characters in their favorite shows are dating it feels like the normal thing to do. A UCLA study found that young adults aged 18 to 24 increasingly want to see less romance in their TV shows and movies, which suggests that the previous generation was saturated with romantic content that shaped their behavior.

None of these reasons are about love. They are about psychological needs that could be met in other ways. A teenager does not need a boyfriend to feel validated and they do not need a girlfriend to feel less lonely. But culture and peer pressure make dating seem like the only solution.

Social Pressure and Media

If biology plants the seed then culture waters it. Teenagers do not grow up in a vacuum. They grow up surrounded by messages that tell them dating is what they are supposed to do.

Start with movies and TV shows. For decades, the central plot of almost every teen movie has been: will they get together. From old romantic comedies to modern Netflix series the message is the same. Your teenage years are incomplete without a love story. This is not harmless entertainment. It shapes expectations. A teenager who watches hundreds of hours of this content genuinely starts to believe that something is wrong with them if they are not in a relationship.

Then there is social media. Platforms like Instagram and TikTok are filled with couples showing off their relationships. One month anniversary posts. Date night videos. How we met stories. This creates constant comparison. A teenager scrolling through this content thinks everyone else has someone so why do not I.

About 60% of teenagers meet their partner through social media according to data from WorldMetrics. And 24% of teenagers who have dated have hooked up with someone they first met online according to Pew Research. Social media does not just reflect teenage dating culture. It actively pushes teenagers into it.

Peer pressure is another huge factor. When your friends start dating you feel left out if you do not. This is basic group psychology. Humans are social animals and teenagers are especially sensitive to social belonging. A 2024 survey found that 44% of Gen Z men who never dated as teenagers reported feeling socially excluded. The pressure to date is not subtle. It is direct and constant.

Parents sometimes add to the pressure too. Questions like do you have a girlfriend yet or when are you going to bring someone home send a clear message. Dating is expected. Even well-meaning parents can turn romance into a milestone that teenagers feel they need to hit.

The result of all this pressure is that teenagers start dating not because they are in love, but because they feel like they are supposed to. the relationship is not driven by genuine connection. It is driven by the fear of being left out.

The Case AgainstTeenage Dating

Now let us get to the uncomfortable part. Most people assume that teenage dating is harmless and just a normal part of growing up. But the data tells a different story.

Academic performance drops. Multiple studies across different countries confirm this. A study published in the International Journal of Chinese Education found that adolescents in romantic relationships had lower academic performance than those who were single. The effect was especially strong for girls. Their academic scores dropped by about 0.270 standard deviations. Research using the National Longitudinal Study of Youth in the U.S. found similar results. Frequent dating and early sexual activity had significant negative effects on both high school graduation rates and college enrollment. When a teenager spends hours texting and calling and meeting up and thinking about a partner there is simply less time and energy left for studying.

Mental health suffers. A study published in the Academic Journal of Psychology and Counseling found that adolescents who date have higher stress levels than those who stay single. Breakups and jealousy and arguments and the fear of being abandoned all take a psychological toll. And because the teenage brain processes emotions more intensely than the adult brain (remember the dopamine spike), these experiences hit harder. Teenagers in relationships report more anxiety and more mood swings and more depressive symptoms. Especially after breakups. Researchers found that currently single adolescents with past dating experiences showed increased depressive symptoms compared to those who never dated at all.

Most teenage relationships are short and pointless. Here is a statistic that nobody talks about. Fewer than 2% of people marry their high school sweetheart. That means 98% of teenage relationships end. They are temporary by nature. So what is the point. Teenagers invest enormous emotional energy into something that has an almost guaranteed expiration date. The question is what are they actually learning from that experience.

Dating violence is real. According to data compiled by WorldMetrics 15% of teenagers have been hit or slapped or physically hurt by a dating partner. About 9.1% of teenagers experience physical dating violence, with girls affected at twice the rate of boys. That is 12.3% vs 5.9%. And 18% of teenagers' first sexual experiences were not fully consensual. These are not small numbers. For a significant percentage of teenagers dating is not a sweet coming of age experience. It is a source of harm.

The alternative nobody considers. Here is the question this paper wants to ask. Why can't teenagers just like each other. Why does attraction have to turn into a formal relationship with labels and expectations and social media announcements. Two teenagers who care about each other can talk every day and support each other and build a deep connection without calling it dating. They can grow up and finish school and develop careers and then decide if they want to be together. The pressure to formalize everything at 14 or 15 is artificial. It comes from culture, not from need.

In many cultures around the world people do not date at all before marriage. They meet and they get to know each other within a social context and they decide to commit when they are ready. This is not oppression. It is patience. And the data suggests that this approach might actually produce better outcomes. A survey by the Survey Center on American Life found that 62% ofAmericans who had a steady partner throughout their teenage years are married today, compared to 46% of those who did not. But correlation is not causation. Having social skills and emotional stability might lead to both. Not the dating itself.

The real issue is that teenagers are not emotionally equipped for the complexity of romantic relationships. Their prefrontal cortex is not developed. Their emotional regulation is weak. Their sense of identity is still forming. Putting them in high pressure romantic situations is like giving someone a driver's license before they even know how to steer.

Conclusion

Teenagers at puberty do have a strong desire to be close to other people, to their opposite gender and peers, but that is another topic. That is real thing we need to accept. Hormones like testosterone, estrogen, dopamine, and oxytocin create powerful feelings of attraction and bonding. This is biology, and nobody can argue with it.

But the leap from attraction to dating is not limited with biology. It is culture. Dating was invented around 100 years ago. For thousands of years before that humans managed to fall in love and build families and create societies without it. The idea that a 14 year old needs to have a boyfriend or girlfriend is a modern invention pushed by movies and social media and peer pressure and a culture that treats romance as a milestone of growing up.

The data shows that teenage dating on average does more harm than good. It lowers academic performance. It increases stress and anxiety. It leads to violence for a disturbing number of teenagers. And 98% of these relationships end anyway.

This does not mean teenagers should suppress their feelings. Feelings are natural. But acting on every feeling is not maturity. It is impulsiveness. A teenager can like someone and enjoy their company and build a genuine connection without the pressure and labels and drama of a formal relationship. They can choose to wait. To grow and to learn and to figure out who they are before jumping into something they are not ready for.

The question is not whether teenagers feel the desire to date. They do. The question is whether we, as a society, should keep encouraging them to act on it without thinking. The evidence suggests we should not.
